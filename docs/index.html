EV rebound in an electrifying fleet

A diffusion model with heterogeneous ICEV disutility, calibrated to the Dutch BEV fleet

Working draft, 27 October 2026

Status. This is a working model. The rebound target (10 log points for the 2015 cohort) is a fictitious placeholder, to be replaced by register-data estimates per cohort. The levels are illustrative. A PDF version of this page is also available.

1 Question

How does the driving rebound of households that switch from an ICEV to a BEV develop over time as the fleet electrifies?

2 Model

2.1 Driving

The model makes one behavioural assumption about heterogeneity: households differ in their disutility from driving an ICEV, for example because of climate concerns. Who switches when, and each cohort’s rebound, follow from optimising behaviour and the observed diffusion of BEVs.

A household ii owns one car. The perceived cost per km is

ciI=pf+ψi(ICEV),cE(EV), c^I_i = p_f + \psi_i \quad\text{(ICEV)}, \qquad c^E \quad\text{(EV)},

where ψi≥0\psi_i \ge 0 is the household’s disutility per km driven with an ICEV. This is the heterogeneous primitive. Demand for km has a common elasticity, vi(c)=ki(c/ciI)−εv_i(c) = k_i (c/c^I_i)^{-\varepsilon}, where kik_i is the household’s km with the ICEV.

The rebound is the log change in km on switching:

ri=εlnciIcE. r_i = \varepsilon \ln\frac{c^I_i}{c^E}.

2.2 Adoption

The gain from switching is the present value of the extra surplus from driving, plus the status that owning an EV gives. Households value this status differently, gi∼N(0,σg2)g_i \sim N(0,\sigma_g^2). Status is positional: it fades as EVs become common, with weight ϕ(St−1)\phi(S_{t-1}) in last year’s BEV share. Status affects the decision to switch, but not how much the household drives:

Git=A∫cEciIvi(c)dc+ϕ(St−1)gi=AkiciI1−ε[1−(cEciI)1−ε]+ϕ(St−1)gi,ϕ(S)=0.5S/S‾. G_{it} = A \int_{c^E}^{c^I_i} v_i(c)\,dc + \phi(S_{t-1})\,g_i = A\,\frac{k_i\,c^I_i}{1-\varepsilon}\left[1-\left(\frac{c^E}{c^I_i}\right)^{1-\varepsilon}\right] + \phi(S_{t-1})\,g_i , \qquad \phi(S) = 0.5^{\,S/\bar S}.

Here:

  • AA is an annuity factor over 88 years at 4%4\%;
  • status halves with every S‾=10\bar S = 10 percentage points of BEV share.

A household can only switch when it replaces its car, which it does with probability λ=0.12\lambda = 0.12 per year. It then switches if Git≥FtG_{it} \ge F_t, where FtF_t is the net EV premium in year tt.

As status fades, the driving gain weighs more in the decision, which sharpens selection on ψ\psi in later cohorts. Up to 2026 the effect is small, because the BEV share stays below 10%.

2.3 What the model implies for rebound over time

Both the gain and the rebound rise in ψi\psi_i:

∂Gi∂ψi=Avi(ciI)>0,∂ri∂ψi=εciI>0. \frac{\partial G_i}{\partial \psi_i} = A\,v_i(c^I_i) > 0, \qquad \frac{\partial r_i}{\partial \psi_i} = \frac{\varepsilon}{c^I_i} > 0 .

Households with a high ICEV disutility therefore switch earlier and rebound more. As FtF_t falls, adoption moves to households with a lower ψ\psi, and the cohort rebound declines.

How strong this is depends on how much of the variation in GG comes from ψ\psi rather than from kk and gg. Those two shift adoption but not the rebound. With a homogeneous ψ\psi, or with adoption timing unrelated to type, rebound is the same for every cohort.

4 Results

4.1 Rebound over time

With a heterogeneous ICEV disutility, the cohort rebound falls from 10.0 log points (adopters up to 2015) to 3.7 (2026). Most of the decline happens before the BEV share reaches about 1% (2019). After that, the profile is nearly flat.

With a homogeneous disutility, or with random timing, the rebound is constant. Each scenario is recalibrated to the same 2015 target.

4.2 Who switches

Early cohorts have a high ICEV disutility and drive a lot. Both fall as the fleet electrifies.

4.3 Untargeted check: km of BEVs over time

The model matches the 2024 ratio. In earlier years, it has BEVs driven far more than the average car, while StatLine shows a modest ratio that rises over time. This points to constraints the model leaves out:

  • the limited range of early BEVs;
  • company cars.

5 Next steps

  1. Target the full rebound profile per cohort from register data. Whether, and how steeply, it declines identifies how dispersed the ICEV disutility is.
  2. Add a range constraint that loosens over time, and a company car segment.
  3. Separate cohort, period and duration effects, using ICEV-to-ICEV replacers as controls.
  4. Compute the rebound of the remaining ICEV owners under a phase-out.
